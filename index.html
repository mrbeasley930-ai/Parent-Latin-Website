What it looks like

A Year 8 lesson in one sentence

Tap each word.

puellathe girl
inin
hortothe gardenhorticulture
ambulatis walkingambulance, amble

The girl is walking in the garden.

Now the part that surprises everyone. Latin does not care about word order. The ending of each word tells you its job in the sentence, so this means exactly the same:

inin
hortothe garden
ambulatis walking
puellathe girl

Try that in English and you get nonsense. Working out how a language carries meaning through endings rather than order is the core skill of Latin, and it is the reason Latin pupils tend to find French, Spanish and German grammar easier when they meet it.

In a typical fortnight

Three Latin lessons, alongside the modern language every pupil studies. New vocabulary, one grammar idea, short stories to translate about a Roman family, and a piece of Roman life — the baths, the games, the army, Pompeii.

The arcade

The department has built its own set of arcade games — a Space Invaders, a Snake, a Pac-Man with a Minotaur — where the only way to score is to get the Latin right. Pupils play them for homework and compete on a school leaderboard. You will see them on the laptops this evening.

The evidence

What Latin does for your child

60% of English words

have Latin or Greek roots, and about 80% of dictionary entries are borrowed, mainly from Latin. A child who knows aqua, manus and credo can unlock aquatic, manual and incredible without a dictionary. This is why the quiz above felt easy.

Source: Dictionary.com, word origins [1]

2.7% of state schools

teach Latin in Years 7–9, against 49% of independent schools. Five Ways is one of the few state schools where your child can study it from Year 8 to GCSE without paying for it.

Source: British Council Language Trends survey, cited by Edapt [2]

Reading and writing in English

A review of 46 studies found that pupils learning Latin mostly outperformed comparison groups on literacy tests, with the strongest effects for younger pupils. Pupils also reported enjoying the connections between Latin and the modern languages they were learning.

Source: Bracke & Bradshaw, The impact of learning Latin on school pupils, Language Learning Journal, 2020 [3]

Modern languages

French, Spanish, Italian and Portuguese grew out of Latin, and their grammar — tenses, agreement, gender — is Latin grammar. Pupils who have met these ideas in Latin recognise them in Year 9 French rather than meeting them cold.

A recognised qualification

GCSE Latin counts as the language in the English Baccalaureate, alongside French, Spanish and German. It is a full academic GCSE with a national grading scale, and it leads to A levels in Latin and Classical Civilisation.

A word of honesty

Some of the claims made for Latin are overstated. A large Belgian study in 2024 found that pupils who choose Latin are often strong readers already, so not every advantage is caused by Latin itself. The finding that survives is vocabulary: Latin pupils kept gaining words year on year. We would rather you heard that from us.

Source: Hauspie et al., Contemporary Educational Psychology, 2024 [4]

Year 8 to Sixth Form

Where it leads

  • Years 8 and 9

    Two form groups study Latin

    Pupils learn the language from the start using Latin to GCSE, alongside Roman history and daily life. By the end of Year 9 they can read a short Latin story unaided.

    servi cenam in villa parant.
    The slaves are preparing dinner in the house.
  • Years 10 and 11

    GCSE Latin (Eduqas)

    Three exam papers: Latin Language (50%) — translating and understanding unseen Latin; Latin Literature and Sources (30%) — real Roman authors on a theme; and either Latin Literature or Roman Civilisation (20%).

    Which word tells you the soldiers had already left the camp? Explain how you know.
    The kind of question a GCSE paper asks: reasoning from the ending of a word.

    Source: Eduqas GCSE Latin specification [5]

  • Sixth Form

    A level Classical Civilisation (OCR)

    Homer's Odyssey, Virgil's Aeneid, Greek art, Greek theatre, love poetry and philosophy — studied in translation, so it is open to pupils who did not take GCSE Latin as well as those who did.

Straight answers

Questions parents ask

Is Latin hard?
It is a proper academic subject and it rewards effort. The early stages are about pattern-spotting, which pupils who have just done the 11+ are good at. The hard parts — longer translation, literature — come gradually over four years, and the department's self-marking online lessons mean nobody gets stuck without knowing it.
How much homework is there?
In Year 8, one piece a week, usually vocabulary practice through the online games or a short translation. It is designed to be done in 20–30 minutes.
Does my child need to know any Latin before Year 8?
No. Everyone starts from nothing, and the course assumes that. There is nothing you need to do before September beyond the ideas at the bottom of this page, and those are optional.
Which form groups do Latin, and can we choose?
Two Year 8 form groups study Latin, three lessons a fortnight alongside their modern language. Which groups do Latin is decided by Mrs Edwards, Head of Languages, not by choice at this stage. If you have a question about how that works, use the form below and we will pass it on.
Does Latin get in the way of French or Spanish?
No. Pupils in the Latin groups study their modern language on the same timetable as everyone else, and in our experience they find its grammar easier because they have already met the ideas in Latin.
What if my child struggles or does not enjoy it?
We track every pupil's work online lesson by lesson, so we see a problem early and step in with targeted practice rather than waiting for a test. Pupils who genuinely do not take to it are not trapped: Latin is chosen or not at GCSE like any other option.
Is Latin useful for a career?
Latin does not lead to one job. What it gives is a strong command of English vocabulary, a habit of careful reading, and the ability to argue from evidence, and universities treat it as a rigorous academic subject. It is no bar to anything: a pupil can take GCSE Latin alongside triple science and go on to medicine or engineering as easily as to law or history.
Isn't Latin a dead language?
Nobody speaks it at home, which is exactly why it is taught the way it is: as a system to be understood rather than phrases to be memorised. That is also why it transfers so well to other languages and to English.
Optional

Three things to do at home

None of these is required. Each takes five minutes and each one makes September easier.

  1. Root of the day. When a long word comes up at the dinner table — transparent, audience, manufacture — ask what the Latin inside it might mean. Trans = across, audio = I hear, manus = hand.
  2. Spot the Latin around you. Coins, the school badge, the words on a courtroom wall, a Harry Potter spell (Lumos = light, Accio = I summon, Nox = night). Children notice it is everywhere once they start looking.
  3. Read the myths. Any retelling of the Greek and Roman myths — Odysseus, Theseus, Aeneas, Romulus. A child who knows the stories arrives in Year 8 already at home in the world the language comes from.
